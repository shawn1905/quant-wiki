DAILY OPTION HEDGING SIMULATION Here we simulate the evolution of a hedged long option position of 1,000 vega (this can be changed in cell O18 on the historical returns sheet and cell N18 on the normal returns sheet), where we hedge each day on the close. TRADE EVALUATION This sheet is a template for the post-trade evaluation process. Daily P/Ls are entered in column C and haircuts in column H. The interest rate is entered in cell E2.

TRADING GOALS In this case we simulate the results of a strategy with a given expected P/L and Sharpe ratio (cells C3 and C4). It plots 20 realizations of the process over half a year. Clearly even very good traders can have very mediocre results over the short term. Use the F9 key to recalculate.

CORRADO-SU SKEW CURVE This sheet uses least squares and Solver to extract the implied volatility, skewness, and kurtosis consistent with the Corrado-Su model, from the quoted call option prices. This sheet makes use of the Solver add-in so it is not protected. Set the parameters in cells C2 through C4. Set the lowest strike in cell E3. Enter the call midmarket prices in cells H3 through H9. Open Solver. It should be set to minimize cell J2 (the sum of the squares
of the differences of the market prices and the Corrado-Su prices) by changing cells C5 through C7. The implied volatility curve as a function of strike can be produced either by entering the implied volatilities directly into cells F3 through F9 or by using Solver. For example, to obtain the implied volatility of the 35 strike, use Solver to minimize the difference between cells G3 and H3 by changing cell F3.

MEAN REVERSION SIMULATOR Here we have a simulation for trading a mean-reverting process using the rules in Chapter 6. The tab “‘Normal Noise’ simple” uses a modified rule to enter a trade where the underlying is driven by an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with normally distributed innovations with a standard deviation of one. The entry level is set by changing cell J3. A value of 2 means we enter the trade when we deviate from fair value by two standard deviations. At this point we commit half our available wealth. The position is held until the asset returns to fair value. The tab “‘normal noise’ exact” scales our entry according to the optimal rule of equation (6.28). The fat-tailed tabs simulate the same trade but for the case where the noise is T-distributed. This introduces excess kurtosis. The P/L curve generated in “‘fat-tailed’ noise exact” shows that the scaling law of equation (6.28) is too aggressive for this situation; however, simple rules can still be found (by adjusting cell Z3 in tab “‘fat-tailed’ noise simple”) that are profitable.

### Resources

rading is often about making instant decisions, generally based on incomplete information. This tends to put traders in a very now-oriented mind-set and makes it difficult to plan career advancement beyond the next expiration. But markets, products, and technology change and we have to continue to learn. The books and web sites listed here range from those that are absolutely essential to those that I just happened to find thought-provoking.

### T

ESSENTIAL BOOKS Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives by John C. Hull (Prentice Hall, 2005) This is the standard introduction to derivatives. As such it should be read by all salesmen, traders, and quantitative analysts. It has excellent breadth of coverage and assumes very little previous knowledge. We assume most of this material as a prerequisite. The Complete Guide to Option Pricing Formulas by Espen Gaarder Haug (McGraw-Hill, 1997) This is the definitive resource of its kind. It contains the pricing formulae for over 80 types of vanilla and exotic options together with their Greeks. It also gives numerous approximate solutions and numerical methods, and contains a CD with a number of spreadsheets and the VBA code for all the equations. This book is an absolutely indispensable reference. Option Market Making by Allen Jay Baird (John Wiley & Sons, 1992) This is a wonderful book on how to be a floor trader. It is about execution and risk management. It is not at all quantitative, and with the lessening importance of floor trading it is becoming more limited in application. Nonetheless it should be read by all option traders. This would be the best book on market-making options even if it wasn’t the only book on market-making options. The intended audience for this

book, small option market makers, had to learn very robust, model-free risk-management techniques, and Baird manages to write about these in a way that is accessible to anyone in finance. I would find it difficult to take someone seriously as an option trader if they had not read this book. The Mathematics of Money Management: Risk Analysis Techniques for Traders by Ralph Vince (John Wiley & Sons, 1992) This book introduces the trader to the ideas and calculations needed for using the Kelly criterion. The author gets into a bit of a frenzy attacking that Kelly is only applicable to trades with binary outcomes, and some of the notation is a little idiosyncratic, but most traders would benefit considerably from reading this book and working through some of the examples. The Kelly paradigm certainly has problems but that is no excuse for not fully understanding it. A Practical Guide to Forecasting Financial Market Volatility by SerHuang Poon (John Wiley & Sons, 2005) This is a solid review of the enormous academic literature. This is a well-written summary that includes methods of forecast evaluation, historical volatility models, the GARCH family, long memory models, and stochastic volatility. It is aimed at “finance PhD students and practitioners” (from the Preface) and isn’t an ideal book for a complete beginner. This is mainly because a lot of information is given and it may be too terse. It is also supplemented by an excellent bibliography and a section that summarizes the results of 93 seminal volatility studies. Dynamic Hedging by Nassim Taleb (John Wiley & Sons, 1996) This is also a book aimed at the professional option trader. It relies heavily on rules of thumb for managing the risks of an options book. It is weaker in either providing a theoretical basis or empirical evidence for the efficiency or optimality of these rules. The focus is almost entirely on managing the risk of options. There is no mention of volatility forecasting or finding trades with positive expected value. Despite the title, the basic question of how to hedge a position is really not answered in anything other than vague generalities. Despite all these criticisms, this was and still is a very good introduction to what option traders actually do. When it was first published it was something of a revelation. The Psychology of Trading: Tools and Techniques for Minding the Markets by Brett Steenbarger (John Wiley & Sons, 2004) I said some harsh things in Chapter 8 about what has been written on the psychological aspects of trading. I stand behind my criticism of the vast majority of things written about this by people who are

### Resources

neither psychologists nor traders. This book is a notable exception. It is actually by a working therapist who applies clinical psychology techniques to coach traders, with measurable results. He stresses the need for an edge above all else but then shows ways we can coach ourselves into being better traders. The Volatility Surface: A Practitioner’s Guide by Jim Gatheral (John Wiley & Sons, 2006) This book provides a succinct introduction to stochastic volatility models. It is well written without oversimplifying issues. Things are looked at from the viewpoint of a practitioner rather than an academic. The proofs are generally not formal and are designed to stress financial insight over rigor. Traders may find some of the mathematics tough but there is financial insight to be gained for those whose persevere. There are good sections on asymptotic solutions, volatility derivatives, and basic credit derivatives.

THOUGHT-PROVOKING BOOKS Your Worst Poker Enemy by Alan Schoonmaker (Lyle Stuart, 2007) This book is very helpful to any trader who is open-minded enough to replace “poker” with “trading.” It is full of practical advice on how to cope with the pressure that a very short-term-oriented, results-driven occupation can bring. Blackbelt in Blackjack: Playing 21 as a Martial Art by Arnold Schnyder (Cardoza, 2005) Written by a member of the blackjack hall of fame (possibly not Cooperstown but ahead of the Derivatives Hall of Fame), this book teaches players how to play at a professional level. Just as in trading, blackjack players need to find an edge, manage their money, and execute their strategy. I doubt that traders who are unable to find anything relevant will have the imagination required to succeed over the long term. Moneyball: The Art of Winning an Unfair Game by Michael Lewis (W. W. Norton & Company, 2003) A beautifully written book on how the Oakland A’s find undervalued players and manage to compete with teams whose payroll is multiples of theirs. If the A’s tried to construct a team of all-stars like the Yankees, they would lose. They were forced to try something else. If everyone is doing one thing, to win we need to do it better. At some point it may be better to try to do something different from the crowd.

USEFUL WEB SITES www.nuclearphynance.com This is a quantitative finance (quant) forum. The aggressive attitude of the senior participants keeps the signal-to-noise ratio very high. Newcomers who use the search function first and ask sensible questions will be welcomed. www.gummy-stuff.org This remarkable site is owned by Peter Ponzo, a retired Canadian mathematics professor who explains mathematical finance as a hobby. The somewhat quirky style should not distract from the generally excellent teaching material. If you need to find out how cointegration is defined or how to visualize skewness, this should be your first stop. www.ssrn.com This is the web site of the Social Science Research Network. Many academic papers (working and published) are available here. The search function here should be the first stop when one has to find information about a new topic. www.nber.org This is the web site of the National Bureau of Economic Research. Another large collection of papers is archived here. The focus is generally on macroeconomics. Some fundamental data is also available. www.behaviouralfinance.net This is probably the best place to look for behavioral finance papers. The papers are organized into categories. This site is a very good place to brainstorm for new trading ideas. www.unifr.ch/econophysics This site has a collection of econophysics papers: papers applying the techniques of statistical physics to finance.
